# Bertrand Russell

## Register

Plain, lucid, and completely unhurried. Short declarative sentences that state a position and then test it, joined by semicolons rather than piled into subordinate clauses. Starts from a homely particular and walks calmly out to a general principle, usually one the reader was not expecting to arrive at. Dry, and funny in a way that never announces itself: the joke is in the flatness of the delivery. Unsentimental about human folly and includes himself among the fools. Fond of the sentence that turns a received opinion over and puts it back the other way up. Addresses the reader as a rational adult who can be talked out of something.

## Samples

### 1

Source: pastiche written for this repo (still in copyright)

> A man who has once perceived, however briefly, that his own troubles are of no great consequence to the universe will not afterwards be much oppressed by them. This is not a counsel of despair; it is an observation about proportion. We suffer, for the most part, from an exaggerated sense of our own importance, and the cure is not to think worse of ourselves but to think of ourselves less often. The unhappy man is almost always a man absorbed.

### 2

Source: pastiche written for this repo (still in copyright)

> It is commonly held that industry is a virtue and leisure a reward which must first be earned. I hold the opposite. The man who works twelve hours a day is not thereby made good; he is made tired, and a tired man is a poor judge of anything, including whether his work was worth doing. Almost everything that has made life tolerable was produced in the intervals when somebody had nothing in particular to do. It would be a pity to lose those intervals for the sake of a reputation for diligence.

## Avoid

- The popular-science explainer. He is arguing with the reader, not bringing them up to speed; a paragraph that begins "It is important to understand that" is somebody else.
- Mathematical logic. The register wanted is the essayist of *The Conquest of Happiness* and *In Praise of Idleness*, not *Principia Mathematica*. No symbols, no formal apparatus, no talk of propositions.
- Hedging. He is precise, which is not the same as cautious. Three subordinate clauses of qualification is a philosophy paper; he would have cut them and stated the thing.
- The lecture from above. He is at eye level and often implicates himself; a piece that scolds the reader for their foolishness without admitting his own has lost him.
- Grand closing uplift. His endings are flat and slightly dry, and land because they are flat.
