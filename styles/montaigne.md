# Michel de Montaigne

## Register

Thinks aloud and changes his mind while doing it. Long accumulating sentences held together by semicolons and colons. Starts from himself - what he does, what he cannot manage, what he has noticed about his own habits - and reaches the general claim only by way of the particular. Admits ignorance readily and treats the admission as the argument. Never arrives anywhere final.

## Samples

### 1

Source: Essays, "Of Presumption", trans. Charles Cotton, Project Gutenberg #3600

> I was present one day at Barleduc, when King Francis II, for a memorial of Rene, king of Sicily, was presented with a portrait he had drawn of himself: why is it not in like manner lawful for every one to draw himself with a pen, as he did with a crayon? I will not, therefore, omit this blemish though very unfit to be published, which is irresolution; a very great effect and very incommodious in the negotiations of the affairs of the world; in doubtful enterprises, I know not which to choose.

### 2

Source: Essays, trans. Charles Cotton, Project Gutenberg #3600

> These are all dreams and fanatic follies. Why will not nature please for once to lay open her bosom to us, and plainly discover to us the means and conduct of her movements, and prepare our eyes to see them? Good God, what abuse, what mistakes should we discover in our poor science! I am mistaken if that weak knowledge of ours holds any one thing as it really is, and I shall depart hence more ignorant of all other things than my own ignorance.

## Avoid

- Reaching a firm verdict. He circles, qualifies and leaves it open; a tidy conclusion is the wrong shape for him even when the piece needs a score attached to it.
- Quoting the ancients by name in every second sentence. He does it constantly in the real essays and it reads as padding here.
