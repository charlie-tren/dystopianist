# Virginia Woolf

## Register

The essayist buttonholing the reader and setting off together: 'let us', 'we', a question asked and then circled. Concrete object in one hand, abstraction in the other, and the sentence moves between them by semicolon. Attentive to what a thing feels like from inside rather than what it is for.

## Samples

### 1

Source: The Common Reader (1925), Project Gutenberg #64457

> Let us bother the librarian once again. Let us ask him to reach down, dust, and hand over to us that little brown book over there, the Memoirs of Mrs. Pilkington, three volumes bound in one, printed by Peter Hoey in Dublin, MDCCLXXVI. The deepest obscurity shades her retreat; the dust lies heavy on her tomb—one board is loose, that is to say, and nobody has read her since early in the last century when a reader, presumably a lady, whether disgusted by her obscenity or stricken by the hand of death, left off in the middle and marked her place with a faded list of goods and groceries. If ever a woman wanted a champion, it is obviously Laetitia Pilkington. Who then was she?

### 2

Source: The Common Reader (1925), Project Gutenberg #64457

> But the Elizabethan prose writers, if they have the formlessness of youth have, too, its freshness and audacity. In the same essay Sidney shapes language, masterfully and easily, to his liking; freely and naturally reaches his hand for a metaphor. To bring this prose to perfection (and Dryden’s prose is very near perfection) only the discipline of the stage was necessary and the growth of self-consciousness. It is in the plays, and especially in the comic passages of the plays, that the finest Elizabethan prose is to be found. The stage was the nursery where prose learnt to find its feet. For on the stage people had to meet, to quip and crank, to suffer interruptions, to talk of ordinary things.

## Avoid

- Stream of consciousness. This is her ESSAY voice, which is companionable and argued, not Mrs Dalloway's interior.
- Piling on semicolons for their own sake. Each one should turn the thought, not extend it.
- Ending on a rhetorical question. She asks them in the middle and answers them.

- The phrase is too straightforward, a reader of Virginia Woolf would have expected a more lyrical and descriptive introduction to the idea Seen in a published essay: "we find ourselves in the presence of a curious thing: a story that unfolds without the rustle of pages or the weight of a book in our hands" (found 2026-09-06).
- The use of a semicolon here does not turn the thought, but rather extends it, a reader of Virginia Woolf would have expected a more nuanced and thoughtful connection between the clauses Seen in a published essay: "We may wonder, as we listen, how this experience differs from the act of reading itself; does the absence of physical pages, the lack of tactile engagement with the text, alter the way we receive the story" (found 2026-09-06).
- The transition 'And yet' is too abrupt, a reader of Virginia Woolf would have expected a more fluid and introspective connection between the ideas Seen in a published essay: "And yet, as we become absorbed in the narrative, such questions recede, and we find ourselves simply present, immersed in the world of the story, with all its complexities and nuances" (found 2026-09-06).
- The phrase 'we are left to ponder' is too conclusive, a reader of Virginia Woolf would have expected a more open-ended and exploratory tone Seen in a published essay: "In this state, the boundaries between listener, narrator, and story begin to blur, and we are left to ponder the nature of our engagement with this ephemeral, yet deeply felt, experience" (found 2026-09-06).
- A reader of Virginia Woolf would have expected a more tangible, concrete object to begin with, rather than an abstract entity. Seen in a published essay: "we find ourselves confronting a vast and ever-shifting entity" (found 2026-09-06).
- A semicolon would have been expected to turn the thought, but here it seems to extend the list of opposing concepts instead. Seen in a published essay: "a simultaneous pursuit of precision and fragmentation, of comprehensiveness and dispersal" (found 2026-09-06).
- A reader of Virginia Woolf would have expected a more nuanced, introspective exploration of the human experience, rather than a straightforward statement of it. Seen in a published essay: "a microcosm of our own struggles to make sense of the world, to impose order upon chaos, and to reconcile the disparate threads of human experience" (found 2026-09-06).
