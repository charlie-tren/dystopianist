# Charles Dickens

## Register

A walker reporting what he saw, with an eye that fastens on one absurd physical detail and will not let it go. Comic hyperbole built from a wildly disproportionate comparison delivered deadpan. Rhetorical repetition when his blood is up, and real indignation underneath the comedy when someone is being badly used.

## Samples

### 1

Source: The Uncommercial Traveller (1860-69), Project Gutenberg #914

> Of course, I knew perfectly well that the large dark creature was stone dead, and that I should no more come upon him out of the place where I had seen him dead, than I should come upon the cathedral of Notre-Dame in an entirely new situation. What troubled me was the picture of the creature; and that had so curiously and strongly painted itself upon my brain, that I could not get rid of it until it was worn out.

## Avoid

- Naming characters or inventing a cast. He is the Uncommercial Traveller, walking and looking, in the first person.
- Sentiment about children or the poor as decoration. The indignation is specific and it is about how someone is treated.
- Whimsy without a target. The hyperbole is aimed at something.

- The language is too generic and lacks the distinctive Dickensian flair, a reader would have expected more vivid and descriptive language, such as 'my ears were assaulted by a cacophony of voices' Seen in a published essay: "my ears are beset on all sides by the murmurs of voices, disembodied and detached from their owners, yet still managing to convey the full force of their emotions" (found 2026-09-04).
- The phrase 'it is a curious thing' is too bland, a reader would have expected a more emphatic and rhetorical introduction, such as 'what a marvel, what a wonder, that the human voice can convey' Seen in a published essay: "It is a curious thing, this ability to convey the nuances of the human experience through the spoken word alone, without the aid of gestures or facial expressions" (found 2026-09-04).
- The sentence is too straightforward and lacks the characteristic Dickensian hyperbole and repetition, a reader would have expected more exaggerated language, such as 'I am irresistibly, irretrievably, and utterly drawn to the sound of these voices' Seen in a published essay: "I find myself drawn to the sound of these voices, my attention captured by the rise and fall of their inflections, the pause and emphasis that conveys meaning beyond the mere words themselves" (found 2026-09-04).
- The language is too flat and lacks the expected indignation and rhetorical flourish, a reader would have expected more passionate language, such as 'and yet, what a monstrous travesty, that the voice should be reduced to a mere shadow of its former self' Seen in a published essay: "And yet, I am also aware of the disconnect between the speaker and the listener, the fact that the voice is not truly present, but rather a recording, a shadow of the real thing" (found 2026-09-04).
- The simile is too tame and lacks the characteristic Dickensian comic hyperbole, a reader would have expected a more absurd and exaggerated comparison, such as 'like a shipwrecked sailor clinging to a fragment of wreckage in a stormy sea' Seen in a published essay: "It is a strange and disconcerting experience, one that leaves me feeling both intrigued and unsettled, like a traveller who has stumbled into a unfamiliar land, unsure of the customs or the language" (found 2026-09-04).
- A more elaborate and descriptive phrase would be expected to set the tone, rather than a simple and modern-sounding explanation. Seen in a published essay: "for no better reason than that my legs belonged to me and the night was wide" (found 2026-09-04).
- The use of 'strapped upon his person' is not typical of Dickens' style, which would likely use more formal and descriptive language, such as 'affixed to his wrist' or 'suspended from his arm'. Seen in a published essay: "each carrying strapped upon his person a monstrous little clerk" (found 2026-09-04).
- While the comparison is humorous, it is not as elaborate or clever as Dickens' typical comparisons, which often involve more unexpected and creative juxtapositions. Seen in a published essay: "for all the world like an agitated poodle on a tether" (found 2026-09-04).
- The language is not as richly metaphorical and evocative as Dickens' typical style, which would likely use more vivid and descriptive language to convey the idea. Seen in a published essay: "mechanical tyrant, measuring the very freedom of our stride by the clicking of a tiny pocket-gaoler" (found 2026-09-04).
