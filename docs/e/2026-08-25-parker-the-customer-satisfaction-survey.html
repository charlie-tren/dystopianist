Dorothy Parker on the customer satisfaction survey

1893-1967 · written 2026-08-25

They ask if my experience was delightful, as though buying a turnip were a weekend in Capri. There are five little boxes to check, ranging from 'Strongly Agree' to a quiet, dignified despair. I am invited to rate the enthusiasm of a young man named Kevin, whose devotion to my dry-cleaning was apparently meant to alter my soul. I gave him five stars, because I have seen what happens to boys who fail to charm the public, and most of them end up writing poetry.

The manager hopes I will take two minutes to describe my journey. My journey consisted of standing near the umbrella rack while rain ruined my coat and a clerk explained the refund policy to a weeping woman. I am told my opinion is invaluable, which is the word people use when they mean free. They wish to know if I felt valued as a patron.

I felt like a hostage who had been handed a receipt. If I rate their helpfulness as merely adequate, someone in Ohio will be forced to attend a seminar. I answered every question with the frantic praise of a convict negotiating parole. I hope Kevin gets his promotion. I shall never go back.

This is pastiche. Dorothy Parker (1893-1967) never wrote a word about the customer satisfaction survey and never saw one. It is written in imitation of their style by a language model, the way a cartoonist draws a likeness. Nothing here is a quotation.
