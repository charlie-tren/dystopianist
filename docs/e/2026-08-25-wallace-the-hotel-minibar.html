In the style of

David Foster Wallace on the hotel minibar

As you stand there, staring at the minibar's compact, overlit interior, it's possible that what you're really looking at is not the array of diminutive bottles and snacks, but rather the apotheosis of a certain kind of consumerist intimacy, one that promises to satisfy your every late-night craving while also, somehow, reinforcing the sense of isolation that drove you to raid the thing in the first place, and yet, even as you're aware of this dynamic, you can't help but feel a twinge of excitement, or maybe it's just anticipation, at the prospect of indulging in these pricey, bite-sized luxuries, which is to say that the minibar, in its very design, seems to be exploiting a deep-seated paradox, one that pits your desire for comfort and convenience against your suspicion of being, well, exploited, and it's this tension, this push-and-pull, that makes the experience of using the minibar feel at once so alluring and so fundamentally unsatisfying, like a whispered secret that's also a lie.

This is pastiche. David Foster Wallace never wrote a word about the hotel minibar and never saw one. It is written in imitation of their style by a language model, the way a cartoonist draws a likeness.
