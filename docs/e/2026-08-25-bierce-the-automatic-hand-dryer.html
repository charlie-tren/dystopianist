In the style of

Ambrose Bierce on the automatic hand dryer

1842-c.1914 · written 2026-08-25

PNEUMATIC DRYER, n. An acoustic terror of public lavatories, devised by mechanical ingenuity to replace the quiet efficacy of the linen towel with thirty seconds of gale-force turbulence and the ultimate necessity of wiping one's wet palms upon one's trousers.

It operates by means of an invisible sensor that detects human presence only after an agonizing period of frantic gesticulation, whereupon it releases a warm, tempestuous blast calculated to scatter moisture from the skin into the surrounding atmosphere, whence it may be inhaled by the helpless bystander. The machine is acclaimed by municipal economists as a supreme triumph of thrift, since it consumes only electricity, public patience, and the hearing of its victim, while leaving the hands precisely as damp as they were before the tempest commenced, though considerably colder and less clean. Thus does modern civilization demonstrate its relentless march: by substituting a deafening clamor for a silent cloth, so that he who sought merely to dry his fingers is returned to society with his dignity diminished, his ears ringing, and his own coat stealthily recruited to fulfill the ancient purpose of the towel.

This is pastiche. Ambrose Bierce (1842-c.1914) never wrote a word about the automatic hand dryer and never saw one. It is written in imitation of their style by a language model, the way a cartoonist draws a likeness. Nothing here is a quotation.
